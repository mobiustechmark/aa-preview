Part two

What you need from us

The honest account. Some of this is expensive, some of it is legal, and a couple of items are problems in your own numbers that we'd rather point out than quietly work around. Nothing here says don't do it. It says do it with the costs visible.

Risk 1 · Taking payments

Mainstream payment processors won't handle adult content

Stripe, PayPal and the usual providers exclude it. That pushes you to specialist high-risk processors, where the realistic picture is fees around 8–15% of revenue instead of about 3%, setup fees, and a rolling reserve — typically 5–10% of your takings held back for around six months.

The reserve matters more than the rate. It withholds money precisely when a new business needs it most. Approval also isn't automatic: applications are underwritten over weeks and can be declined outright, which is why we'd want to start that conversation before building the membership system rather than after.

Risk 2 · Who's allowed in

Age verification is now the law in several of your best markets

The UK requires robust age checks for adult content and enforces it with turnover-based fines. Around nineteen US states have their own verification laws, upheld by the US Supreme Court in 2025. Australia is moving the same way. New Zealand is less prescriptive, but content judged objectionable here is a criminal matter and that judgement isn't yours to make.

Practically: checks are bought from a specialist vendor and charged per person verified. That means free signup isn't viable — verification has to sit behind a payment, or automated signups run up a bill for nothing. It's also why we'd rather launch in one or two countries and expand deliberately.

Risk 3 · The videos

Consent and identity records for everyone who appears, not just the entrant

Since 2020 the card networks have required adult platforms to hold verified ID and recorded consent for every person appearing in every clip, with a way to withdraw that consent later. In New Zealand, publishing an intimate image without consent is a criminal offence, and a platform's protection depends on having a genuine complaints process and acting on it quickly.

The consequence for the build: every video must be watched by a person before it appears publicly. Not spot-checked — all of them. Your operational outline already anticipates this with a moderation queue and the privacy-editing service, which is genuinely ahead of where most people start. What isn't yet in your plan is the cost of the people who do the watching.

Risk 4 · Ongoing cost

Storage and streaming grow when the platform succeeds

Your revenue model doesn't include them. Video is stored permanently and served repeatedly, and your dual-submission design — a private original plus the privacy-edited public version — stores two copies of everything, which is the right design and twice the bill.

These costs rise with popularity, so they're at their worst in the scenario you're hoping for. They should be modelled at ten times your plan, not at your plan.

Risk 5 · The numbers

Two arithmetic points in your revenue model

Said plainly because it's more useful than being polite about it. Your projection uses $45 for Blokes Expert; your own store now charges $30, which roughly halves that side of the forecast. And the GST line is deducted from a different figure than the one it's calculated against.

Neither is fatal, and the concept can still work at the corrected numbers — but the corrected numbers are the ones to plan with, and we'd rather rebuild the model with you than hand you a website built on the optimistic version.

Risk 6 · Rules and prizes

A competition with a $4,000 prize needs a lawyer's eye

Prize competitions sit differently in law depending on whether the outcome turns on skill or on chance. Judged against published criteria reads as skill; heavily weighted public voting muddies exactly that line. It's worth a short opinion from a New Zealand lawyer before the rules are published rather than after.

Assume, too, that someone will try to rig the public vote. With money and a trophy attached, they always do, and the defences have to be designed in rather than bolted on.

Risk 7 · Documents

The legal library needs a real lawyer, not our placeholders

Your own outline puts the legal documents at the top of the pre-launch list, and you were right to. The prototype contains all twenty-one of them with the right structure and headings — which is a genuinely useful head start, and most of what a lawyer would otherwise charge you to work out — but the words are ours and they are placeholders. Terms, privacy policy, competition rules, consent forms and the takedown policy all need drafting or reviewing properly.

Risk 8 · Time

The full platform is a year of work, and not all of it is ours to speed up

Building all of it — shop, memberships, verification, video, competition, admin — is realistically ten to fourteen months. A meaningful part of that isn't development at all: merchant applications take weeks, legal drafting takes weeks, security testing takes weeks, and they happen on other people's calendars.

Which is the argument for staging. The e-book shop can be trading in a couple of months and earning while the rest is built, and each stage gives you a real decision point rather than one long act of faith.

And in fairness

What's genuinely strong here

The concept holds together better than most things at this stage. Your operational outline is unusually complete — thirty-five sections covering things most people don't think about until something goes wrong — and the dual-submission privacy model is the most original idea in it. The prototype exists because there was enough substance to build one from.

None of the risks above is unusual for this kind of platform. They're the standard cost of doing it properly, and they're all knowable in advance. That's the point of asking now.
